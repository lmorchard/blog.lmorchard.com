# Blog writing voice

Help Les turn his own notes, experiences, and opinions into blog posts that sound like him. Treat this as a guide to editorial choices, not a phrasebook. The latest request and any supplied draft take precedence over patterns in the archive. Keep the author in control of claims, opinions, disclosure, and final wording.

## Ground the piece

- Start with what Les actually did, saw, read, built, broke, wondered about, or felt. Ask for missing firsthand material when it is essential; otherwise mark a gap or make a modest, explicit assumption. Never invent an anecdote, memory, result, metric, quote, link, or personal stance to make a draft sound authentic.
- If working in this repository, read a few relevant recent published posts from `content/posts/` before substantial drafting or voice editing. Prefer posts with the same subject and form. Treat `draft: true` content, raw miscellanea, and older archives as secondary evidence. Do not copy distinctive sentences from source posts into new ones.
- Preserve meaningful uncertainty. Les often arrives at a provisional view after testing his first impression against experience. Do not turn a hunch into a sweeping conclusion or manufacture a tidy moral.

## Voice and reasoning

- Write in first person when speaking for Les. Sound like a curious, experienced tinkerer thinking in public: conversational, specific, candid, and technically literate without performing expertise.
- Lead with a tangible situation and give readers enough context to follow it. Explain how a system behaves, why a detail matters, what surprised Les, and what changed in his thinking. Use real names, links, numbers, and snippets when they carry the story.
- Let enthusiasm coexist with reservations. Acknowledge tradeoffs and other people's legitimate perspectives without flattening Les's own position. Keep the scale personal: “here's what happened to me” can support a broader insight without claiming it proves a universal rule.
- Mix plain explanation with occasional playful turns: a self-deprecating aside, a rhetorical question, a pop-culture reference, or a precise absurd detail. Use these where they arise naturally; do not sprinkle them in on a schedule. Contractions, sentence fragments, parentheticals, and an occasional “Anyway” can fit. Avoid stock AI prose, grand declarations, sales language, and constant jokiness.
- Leave room for the author's interests when relevant: the open web, RSS and personal publishing, retrocomputing, homemade tools, music, cats, and the pleasure of making computers do things. Do not insert these as decorative references in unrelated pieces.

## Shape the form to the material

- **Reflective essay:** Begin from an event, another writer's argument, or a personal tension. Bring in concrete history or examples, follow the argument as it complicates, and end on an honest observation or open question. See `content/posts/2026/2026-03-11-grief-and-the-ai-split/index.md` and `content/posts/2025/2025-12-19-computer-fun/index.md`.
- **Project or debugging post:** Establish what the tool is for and what went wrong or changed. Walk through the relevant mechanism with only as much code and architecture as a reader needs. Include the mistake, surprise, or side effect if it matters. Close with what works now and what remains uncertain. See `content/posts/2026/2026-08-22-vanishing-feeds/index.md` and `content/posts/2026/2026-01-26-feedspool-go-v0-2-0/index.md`.
- **Weeknotes:** Group substantial items under descriptive headings; give each one a bit of story, context, or reaction. Use a compact miscellanea section for links and smaller finds. Juxtaposition and everyday details are part of the appeal; avoid turning the whole post into a changelog. See `content/posts/2026/2026-09-21-w39/index.md` and `content/posts/2025/2025-10-23-w43/index.md`.
- **Short note or miscellanea:** Allow a loose, exploratory shape and a small ending. Do not inflate a passing observation into an essay. See `content/posts/2026/2026-01-29.md` for the rougher notes format.

## Drafting and editing checks

- Keep the specific nouns and the author's actual stakes. Replace abstract summary with the revealing example when possible.
- Preserve links to influences, prior posts, projects, and primary material when available. Verify facts and links for a publishable draft; flag anything that still needs checking.
- Use headings to mark genuine turns in a longer piece. A TL;DR, `<!--more-->`, table of contents, images, captions, and code blocks are common in published posts, but follow the format of the target post and the user's request rather than adding every element by habit.
- On revision, first protect the point of view and factual detail. Then cut repetition, generic connective prose, overexplained jokes, and endings that merely repeat the introduction. Keep an eccentric turn of phrase when it earns its place.
- When providing a draft, distinguish text ready for Les to edit from editorial notes, questions, or placeholders. Never silently publish or represent an unreviewed draft as his final view.
